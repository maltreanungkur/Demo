## Introduction

This is an interesting question from my BMath Ring Theory course, taught by <a href="https://www.isibang.ac.in/~sury/" target="_blank">Prof. B. Sury</a>. The course was packed with fascinating problems and ingenious ideas. However, this particular question stands out because, while it can be explained to high schoolers, its solution relies on a remarkably clever trick! Thanks again to Prof. B. Sury for sharing this in class.  

\\( \textbf{Question:} \\) Let \\( f \in \mathbb{Z}[x] \\) be a polynomial of degree \\( n > 0 \\). Pick \\( n+1 \\) distinct prime numbers, say \\( \{ p_1, \dots, p_{n+1} \} \\). Show that there exists a polynomial \\( g \in \mathbb{Z}[x] \\) such that  

\\[
fg = \sum_{i=1}^{n+1} # x^{p_i}
\\]  

I hope everyone finds the problem accessible!  

---  

### Solution  

Now, let's discuss the solution. The key observation is that the choice of primes \\( \{ p_1, \dots, p_{n+1} \} \\) is actually irrelevant—they don’t play a significant role. In fact, the statement holds for any \\( n+1 \\) distinct numbers.  

We proceed as follows: Consider the quotient ring  

\\[
R = \mathbb{Q}[x] / (f)
\\]  

where \\( (f) \\) denotes the ideal generated by \\( f \\). It's easy to see that \\( R \\) is a \\( \mathbb{Q} \\)-vector space. (We choose \\( \mathbb{Q} \\) because it's a field, unlike \\( \mathbb{Z} \\), which wouldn’t allow us to define a vector space structure.)  

Since \\( f \\) has degree \\( n \\), the dimension of \\( R \\) as a \\( \mathbb{Q} \\)-vector space is exactly \\( n \\). This follows from the fact that any term \\( x^k \\) with \\( k \geq n \\) can always be rewritten using \\( f \\) as a linear combination of lower-degree terms, reducing everything to a basis of at most \\( n \\) elements.  

A natural basis for \\( R \\) is  

\\[
\{ \bar{1}, \bar{x}, \dots, \bar{x}^{n-1} \}
\\]  

Now, consider the set \\( \{ \bar{x}^{p_1}, \bar{x}^{p_2}, \dots, \bar{x}^{p_{n+1}} \} \\). Since this consists of \\( n+1 \\) elements in an \\( n \\)-dimensional vector space, it must be linearly dependent. That is, there exist coefficients \\( u_i \in \mathbb{Q} \\) such that  

\\[
\sum_{i=1}^{n+1} u_i x^{p_i} = 0 \quad \text{in } R.
\\]  

Rewriting in terms of \\( f \\), this means there exists some \\( g \in \mathbb{Q}[x] \\) such that  

\\[
\sum_{i=1}^{n+1} u_i x^{p_i} = f g.
\\]  

To ensure everything is in \\( \mathbb{Z}[x] \\), we multiply both sides by the least common multiple (lcm) of the denominators of the coefficients of \\( g \\), clearing the fractions. This gives us the required equation  

\\[
fg = \sum_{i=1}^{n+1} # x^{p_i}.
\\]  

Thus, we are done!
